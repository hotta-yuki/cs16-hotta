#### Abstract

In acoustics, the term Wood anomaly, in analogy to the Wood anomaly in optics, has so far referred to the anomalies observed in the specular reflection spectra of acoustic waves perpendicularly incident on periodic surfaces. Inspired by the pioneering work of Jungman et al. on the study of the transmission field of a solid-fluid periodic interface, this work attempts to provide a complete experimental investigation of the transmission fields of a broadband sound pulse transmitted through a periodic liquid-solid interface as well as a periodic solid-liquid interface. At different frequencies, two types of anomalies are observed: a spectral tip and a spectral dip, which correspond, respectively, to the brighter band and the darker band in optical Wood anomalies. The search for their physical origin suggests that the type and location of the observed spectral anomalies are strongly related to the generation and the diffraction of pseudosurface waves on the interface having superimposed periodic corrugations and time-domain windowing in spectral analysis. To compare with the surface waves on a plane surface, the properties of the pseudosurface waves are also investigated through examining their phase and by comparing their amplitudes. ## I. INTRODUCTION

Historically speaking, the term “Wood anomaly” refers to the optical phenomenon first observed by Wood, namely, that a brighter band and a darker band appear in the diffraction spectrum of an incident white beam, ${ }^{1}$ as a result of an optical grating. Around three decades ago, when the reflection spectrum of a wideband acoustic wave perpendicularly incident upon a periodically corrugated interface was examined, a sharp dip was observed and discussed. ${ }^{2-6}$ Due to its similarity to the darker band of the Wood anomaly, this acoustic phenomenon received the label “Wood anomaly,” and was later specified as the “acoustic Wood anomaly.” ${ }^{7-9}$ Recently, a spectral tip, as the counterpart of the brighter band of the optical Wood anomaly, has been observed by our team, in acoustics, in the reflection field. ${ }^{8}$ Up to now, most of the investigations on the acoustic Wood anomaly have been performed in the reflection field, and little study has been performed in the transmission field. Inspired by the pioneering work of Jungman et al. ${ }^{10}$ on the study of the transmission field of a solid-fluid periodic interface, here we present the experimental results on the spectral anomalies in the transmission spectrum of a wideband sound beam incident onto both periodic solid-liquid and liquid-solid interfaces, aiming at providing a relatively complete study of the phenomenon of acoustic Wood anomalies in the transmission field.

It is interesting to dig into the history of optics. Concerning the physical origin of the optical Wood anomaly, Rayleigh proposed the first explanation in his notes on this phenomenon ${ }^{11}$ soon after it was discovered; later, he tried to further develop his theory in his famous dynamic theory of gratings. ${ }^{12}$ He conjectured that a spectral anomaly is expected at a particular wavelength of light if the higher order of that wavelength is emerging tangentially to the grating surface. Rayleigh’s theory indeed explained the locations of some of the experimentally observed anomalies with acceptable discrepancy and remained unquestioned for decades until experimental measurements performed on the gratings with the same period but for different materials showed that the location of the maxima of the bright bands shifted among the gratings tested, ${ }^{13}$ a phenomenon that could in fact not be explained by Rayleigh’s conjecture. ${ }^{14}$ Later, during the second world war, the experimentally observed optical Wood anomalies have been further characterized as being of two distinguishable types: ${ }^{15}$ (i) sharp anomalies whose location can be perfectly predicted by Rayleigh’s explanation and (ii) diffuse anomalies whose location is related to both the geometry and the optical properties of the grating materials instead of being only related to the grating geometry as in Rayleigh’s explanation. The diffuse anomalies were believed to be the result of the excitation of surface waves propagating along the grating surface, ${ }^{15,16}$ and their shape and location were numerically explored based on different models. ${ }^{16,17}$ Further investigations of the origin of optical Wood anomalies finally lead to the theory of the excitation of surface plasmon polarizations (SPPs). ${ }^{18,19}$ Now, the excitation of SPP (i.e., surface electromagnetic waves) in the process of diffraction is the widely accepted theory for the origin of optical Wood anomalies. ${ }^{20-24}$

In acoustics, a numerical investigation on the sharp and diffuse anomalies generated on periodically corrugated surfaces has recently been published; ${ }^{25}$ conclusions similar to their optical counterparts have been made concerning their physical origins. In all the known experimental investigation of the acoustic Wood anomaly, Rayleigh’s theory based on the classical grating equation is still valid in determining the anomaly locations. Most researchers believe that surface waves are generated on the periodic surface, and they are the
direct cause of the spectral anomalies experimentally observed. In the framework of existing theories, the current work, in addition to the reported observation of spectral anomalies in transmission, also provides a relatively complete explanation for the observed anomalies.

Based on the existing results, a brief comparison of the optical and acoustic Wood anomalies in terms of their basic properties and physical origins can be summarized as follows. First, Wood anomalies have different types in optics and in acoustics. In optics, Wood anomalies have been categorized into two groups according to their shapes: ${ }^{15}$ (i) sharp anomalies, which appear as a sharp change of intensity amplitude, and (ii) diffuse anomalies, which span over a range of wavelengths (or frequencies) and normally consist of a minimum and maximum intensity. Sharp anomalies are also called Rayleigh anomalies ${ }^{22,24,26-29}$ because Rayleigh’s conjecture that an anomaly will occur at the wavelength (frequency) at grazing emergence in spectra of higher order can accurately predict their positions (frequencies); while diffuse anomalies are often referred to as Wood anomalies in the literature. In acoustics, only sharp anomalies have been experimentally observed, and Rayleigh’s explanation based on the classical grating equation is still valid for predicting the anomaly locations. Second, the physical origin of the optical Wood anomaly is much more complicated than the acoustical Wood anomaly. For the optical Wood anomaly, all the grating properties (period, shape, and material) together with the polarization of the incident light (electromagnetic wave) have severe effects on the type, location and shape of the resulting spectral anomalies. The widely accepted theory behind the optical Wood anomalies is surface wave generation, which relates to surface plasmon polarization; and Rayleigh’s explanation is just a special case of the theory of surface wave generation. While for the acoustic Wood anomaly, Rayleigh’s theory is still capable of predicting the anomaly locations and the generating condition of the surface waves, which are responsible for the anomalies and have been identified for different types of interfaces (solid-liquid, liquid-solid, and solid-air interfaces) with different crosssectional profiles (rectangular, triangular, and sinusoidal). The major difference in the origin of the optical Wood anomaly compared with the acoustic Wood anomaly is the role of the grating material and the incident wave properties. For the optical Wood anomaly, metal is the common grating material, and its optical properties (normally represented using the complex refractive index) are one of the determining factors for the anomaly properties. The different polarizations (either p-polarization or s-polarization) of incident light (electromagnetic wave) interact with metal gratings differently, resulting in different anomaly properties. For the acoustic Wood anomaly, the longitudinal wave is the major wave type that interacts with periodic (grating) structures, and interaction is limited to mechanical interaction without the complexity of interaction of electric and magnetic fields with plasmons in metal gratings.

Although the theory of surface acoustic wave generation on periodic interfaces seems to provide a satisfactory explanation for acoustic Wood anomalies in either reflection or transmission fields, it is still not clear if these surface waves possess the same properties as those generated on a plane interface. Through comparison of the phase and the amplitude of the surface acoustic waves generated on the solidliquid interface and on the liquid-solid interface, the current work also sheds some light on this topic.

The outline of this paper is as follows. First, the details of the experimental investigation are described. Then, the observation of spectral anomalies in the transmission field is presented in the contexts of the spectral analysis of the transmitted signals, which are windowed in the time domain using different methods. Based on the spectral analysis in this work and the previous work on the anomalies in the reflection field, a relatively complete explanation of the physical origin of acoustic Wood anomalies is given. The phase and amplitude properties of the surface waves generated on the solid and the liquid side of the periodic interface are discussed.

## II. EXPERIMENTS

## A. Experimental setup

A brass block, which has a flat surface on one side and a periodically corrugated region on the opposite side, is investigated in this work. The corrugated region of the sample surface contains one-dimensional grooves with a rectangular cross-section profile of $185 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ in period and $25 \mu \mathrm{~m}$ in height. ${ }^{8}$ The corrugated region has a length of 25 mm and 26 mm in the direction parallel to and perpendicular to the grooves, respectively. The total thickness of the sample, i.e., the distance between the corrugated and smooth surfaces, is 11 mm.

The experiments are performed in water using a pair of “NDT System” 20 MHz immersion transducers. The through-transmission mode is applied to obtain the transmission for normal incidence. In the measurements, the distance between the transmitting transducer and the sample is 90 mm , and this produces an ultrasound beam with 5 mm diameter, which is defined by 4% spatial peak intensity, ${ }^{30}$ at the upper surface of the sample. Compared with the size of the corrugated region, this beam is small enough to eliminate the effects of the reflections from the boundary of the corrugated region on the transmissions. In addition, the total thickness of the sample is large enough to exclude the reflections generated within the sample from the transmitted signal selected for analysis.

To check the effect of the direction of the wave propagation on the transmission spectrum, sound pulses propagating through both periodically corrugated liquid-solid and solidliquid interfaces are captured for analysis as depicted in Figs. 1(a) and 1(b), respectively. For the purpose of comparison and to offer a reference, the transmission through smooth interfaces (i.e., a flat surface on both sides) is also obtained in the two configurations of Fig. 1. All the measurements are performed on a customer-designed scanner provided by “Inspection Technology Europe BV.” The angular resolution of this scanner is 0.01°, and this ensures that both the emitter and the receiver are perfectly perpendicular to the sample surfaces during the measurement. In the acquisition of all the transmitted signals, a sampling frequency of 200 MHz and a number of averages of 512 (corresponding to $2.56 \mu \mathrm{~s}$ ) are used. The major operation of processing these signals is the application of the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm to obtain their amplitude spectra as well as phase spectra. To examine the temporal evolution of the frequency components of the received signals, their spectrograms are also obtained. In all processing, a rectangular window is applied.

To demonstrate the time-domain waveforms analyzed in this work, the transmissions through both the periodic liquidsolid interface and its corresponding smooth interface are shown in Fig. 2(a). By a close examination, it can be observed that before $142.3 \mu \mathrm{~s}$ (also shown in Fig. 2(b)), the two transmission waveforms are almost identical, but after $142.3 \mu \mathrm{~s}$ (also shown in Fig. 2(c) with a vertical scale 10 times as much as that in Fig. 2(a)), they have a distinct difference: the smooth interface transmission is almost flat, while the transmission of the periodic liquid-solid interface has a series of oscillations. ## C. Physical origin of the spectral anomalies

To clearly explain the physical origin of the spectral anomalies in the transmission field of the periodically corrugated interface, the physical mechanism leading to their occurrence can be split into three separate processes: (i) generation of pseudosurface waves through diffraction of the incident wave, (ii) diffraction of the generated pseudosurface waves by interface corrugations, and (iii) interference of the diffracted pseudosurface waves with the direct transmission. The schematics of the first two steps of these physical processes for the periodic liquid-solid and solid-liquid interfaces are shown in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b), respectively.

## 1. Generation of pseudosurface waves

When an ultrasonic wave is incident onto a flat interface between two media, specular reflection and transmission will occur, and their directions can be described by Snell’s law. When an ultrasonic wave is incident upon a periodically corrugated interface, however, in addition to specular reflection and transmission, diffracted waves can be generated in both reflection and transmission fields. For normal incidence shown in Fig. 1, the incidence angle is zero degree, i.e., $\theta^{i}=0^{\circ}$ or $\sin \theta^{i}=0$, and the classical grating equation can be simplified as

$$
\sin \theta_{m}^{d}=m \frac{c^{d}}{\Lambda f} .
$$

For a wideband incident wave, Eq. (3) theoretically permits the generation of surface waves through diffraction at some frequencies along the interface ( $\theta_{m}^{d}= \pm 90^{\circ}$ or $\sin \theta_{m}^{d}= \pm 1$ ), the velocities of which can be determined by

$$
c^{d}= \pm \frac{\Lambda f}{m} .
$$

Normally, it is unknown if a surface wave (corresponding to a specific frequency) is merely virtual or may actually physically exist, but if the speed of such a surface wave equals the speed of a known surface wave existing on the corresponding flat interface (a velocity matching), it is very probable that a similar surface wave can be generated on the corrugated interface. ${ }^{2,3,6,10}$

For the brass sample investigated in this study, the existence of two surface waves, notably a Rayleigh wave and a Scholte-Stoneley wave, has been established on the flat interface, and their speeds are $2050 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{8}$ and $1472 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s},{ }^{31}$ respectively. Assuming that surface waves were generated on the periodic interface at the spectral anomaly frequencies (8.19 MHz, 10.91 MHz, and 16.39 MHz) as illustrated in Fig. 3, the speeds of these surface waves should be 1515 m/s, 2018 m/s, and 1516 m/s, respectively, according to Eq. (4) by choosing proper values for the diffraction order $m$. Because these speeds are only $2.9 \%,-1.6 \%$, and $2.9 \%$ different from the Scholte-Stoneley wave, the Rayleigh wave and again the Scholte-Stoneley wave, respectively, it is reasonable to believe that surface waves similar to the Scholte-Stoneley wave are generated on the corrugated interface at 8.19 MHz and 16.39 MHz. Equally, surface waves similar to Rayleigh waves are generated at 10.91 MHz. In order to differentiate the surface waves generated on the periodic interface from the conventional surface acoustic waves generated on the flat interface, these waves have in the literature been called either pseudosurface waves, ${ }^{10}$ surface-type waves, ${ }^{3,6}$ or mode converted surface waves. ${ }^{2,6}$ As a summary, in the physical process described above, a broadband incident bulk wave is converted through diffraction (by surface corrugations) into pseudosurface acoustic waves at the anomaly frequencies.

## 2. Diffraction of the pseudosurface waves

When the above mentioned pseudosurface waves propagate along the periodic interface, they can equally be diffracted by the surface corrugations (through a secondary diffraction ${ }^{30}$ ) just as the diffraction of the incident wideband wave (the primary diffraction), from which they were generated. In the secondary diffraction, the incident angles $\theta^{i}$ are $\pm 90^{\circ}\left(\sin \theta^{i}= \pm 1\right)$, and the classical grating equation (Eq. (1)) can be expressed as

$$
k^{d} \sin \theta_{m}^{d}=m \frac{2 \pi}{\Lambda} \mp k^{i} .
$$

Considering $k=\omega / c$ and $\omega=2 \pi f$ together with Eq. (4) while keeping in mind that the incident wave in this step is the diffracted wave in the first diffraction process, the wavenumber of the incident wave (which is here the pseudosurface wave) can be written as

$$
k^{i}= \pm m \frac{2 \pi}{\Lambda}
$$

and then Eq. (5) can be expanded as

$$
k^{d} \sin \theta_{m}^{d}=m \frac{2 \pi}{\Lambda} \mp\left( \pm m \frac{2 \pi}{\Lambda}\right)
$$

or

$$
\sin \theta_{m}^{d}=0 .
$$

This means that the directions of the diffracted pseudosurface waves are perpendicular to the corrugated interface, which reveals reciprocity, and the diffracted waves can, therefore, propagate into both media forming reflection diffraction ( $\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{S S}}$ and $\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{R}}$ ) and transmission diffraction ( $\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{S S}}$ and $\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{R}}$ ) as shown in Fig. 6.

This physical process is actually a reverse process of the generation of the pseudosurface waves, and in this diffraction process, the generated pseudosurface waves are converted back to bulk waves. The difference between the direct transmission (symbolized by T in Fig. 6) and the transmission diffraction $\left(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{S S}}\right.$ and $\left.\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{R}}\right)$ is that the transmission diffraction contains only the anomaly frequency components, while the direct transmission contains as many frequency components as the original incident wave.

## 3. Interference of the pseudosurface waves with direct transmission

As shown in Fig. 6, both the transmission diffraction $\left(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{S S}}\right.$ and $\left.\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{R}}\right)$ and the direct transmission (T) can be received by the receiving transducer. In the frequency domain representation of the total received signals as in Fig. 3, the interference effects of these two types of waves appear. The type of interference effect is determined by the phase relationship between the diffracted pseudosurface waves ( $\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{S S}}$ and $\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{R}}$ ) and the directly transmitted waves (T) at the corresponding anomaly frequencies. If the phase difference is larger than $\pi /$ 2, an amplitude reducing effect will occur resulting in a spectral dip; if the phase difference is smaller than $\pi / 2$, an amplitude enhancing effect will occur resulting in a spectral tip.

In the current experiments, for either liquid-solid or solid-liquid interface transmission, the spectral dips at 8.19 MHz and 16.39 MHz suggest that the pseudoScholteStoneley waves have a phase difference larger than $\pi / 2$ from the direct transmission, while the spectral tip at 10.91 MHz suggests that the pseudoRayleigh wave has a phase difference smaller than $\pi / 2$ from the direct transmission. These conclusions can be supported by the comparison (difference) of the transmission phase spectra of the periodic interfaces and their corresponding smooth surfaces shown in Fig. 7. For both the periodic liquid-solid interface (Fig. 7(a)) and the periodic solid-liquid interface (Fig. 7(b)), a sharp phase change appears around the anomaly frequency of 8.19 MHz (corresponding to the pseudoScholte-Stoneley wave), while

In addition, a sharp phase shift is expected at 16.39 MHz, but none can be clearly observed. This is reasonable when considering the fact that the pseudoScholte-Stoneley wave $\left(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{s s}}\right)$ generated at this frequency is extremely weak compared with the direct transmission (T).

In addition, it should be pointed out that the interference between the diffracted pseudosurface waves ( $\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{S S}}$ and $\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{R}}$ ) and the direct transmission (T) in this study is different from the conventional wave interference that would, for instance, occur in a two-slit experiment. In the conventional wave interference, the waves involved coexist in space and time and therefore experience physical interaction. However, in the current study, the diffracted pseudosurface waves, which appear as oscillating tails as in Fig. 2(c) for the liquid-solid transmission, reach the receiving transducer at a later time than direct transmission (appearing as main bangs as in Fig. 2(b)). The separation of the main bang and the oscillating tail can also be confirmed by comparing the smooth interface transmission and liquid-solid interface transmission in the time-frequency domain (Figs. 4(a) and 4(b)). Since the main bang and the oscillating tail in either the liquid-solid transmission or the solid-liquid transmission are separated in time and space, their interference effect should not be a real one but a virtual one ${ }^{8}$ resulting from the signal windowing in the signal processing as shown in Fig. 5. As discussed in Section III B, if only the main bang or the oscillating tail is windowed for spectral analysis, the spectral anomalies, i.e., a tip as a result of the amplitude enhancing effect and a dip as a result of the amplitude reducing effect, will not appear as those shown in Fig. 3. In summary, the occurrence of spectral anomalies is highly dependent on the time-domain signal windowing in the spectral analysis.

Moreover, if the incident wave were a continuous wave, the diffracted pseudosurface waves ( $\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{S S}}$ and $\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{R}}$ ) and the direct transmission (T) would overlap in both time and space. In this case, the spectrum of any section (except for the very beginning) of the transmitted signal should contain the same types of spectral anomalies as shown in Fig. 3, but they are resulting from real interferences instead of virtual ones. Note, however, that in the case of harmonic excitation, one would also generate standing wave patterns that may even include standing waves between the transducer and the sample, and therefore, such an experiment is not practical, which is why diffraction experiments at normal incidence are always reported for incident pulses rather than for harmonic excitation.

## D. Characteristics of the generated pseudosurface waves

The main reason that the surface waves generated on the periodic interfaces are called pseudosurface waves is that their actual properties, except for speed, are still unknown. It is not clear if these pseudosurface waves have the same characteristic properties as the conventional surface waves (existing on a flat surface). In this section, two of the characteristic properties of these pseudosurface waves, namely, phase and amplitude, will be examined based on the experimental observations.

## 2. Amplitude of the pseudosurface waves

It is known that at plane solid-liquid (or liquid-solid) interfaces, both Rayleigh waves and Scholte-Stoneley waves have non-symmetric energy distribution: most energy of the Rayleigh wave is located in the solid medium, while most energy of the Scholte-Stoneley wave is in the liquid medium. If the pseudosurface waves generated on the corrugated interfaces have the same energy distribution properties as the plane interface waves, uneven distribution is also expected in the experiments. However, the comparison of the tails (where the energy of the pseudosurface waves is located) of transmission waves through both the liquid-solid interfaces and the solid-liquid interfaces shown in Fig. 9(a) and their corresponding spectra in Fig. 9(b) suggests, because both the sides are responsible for a seemingly equal energy rediffraction, that the energy in both the pseudoRayleigh wave and the pseudoScholte-Stoneley wave distributes evenly in both media. This is a plausible interpretation of the measured results although much research would be required to produce conclusive evidence. ## IV. CONCLUSIONS

This work reports the experimental investigation of the so-called acoustic Wood anomaly phenomenon in the transmission field. Two types of spectral anomalies, i.e., a spectral tip and a spectral dip, are observed from the transmission field of both periodic liquid-solid and solid-liquid interfaces. This work also provides a relatively complete explanation of the physical origin of the transmission spectral anomalies based on Rayleigh’s conjecture and the theory of surface wave generation. The explanation clearly shows the effect of the timedomain windowing on the appearance of the spectral anomalies and points out that the anomalies originate from a virtual interference instead of a real one between the direct transmission (main bang) and the diffracted pseudosurface waves (tail). The properties of the surface waves generated on periodic surfaces are also investigated aiming at a meaningful comparison with those generated on plane interfaces.
